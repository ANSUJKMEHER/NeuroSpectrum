Learning Spectral Point Distributions with Neural Energy Fields
A standalone research proposal ΓÇö v3, detailed Domain: Computer Graphics ΓÇö Procedural Point Distributions, Sampling, Neural Energy Models
Executive Summary
The core system is a dynamical system whose only driver of particle motion is a learned energy field: a small MLP takes pairwise distance (and a target-
spectrum condition) as input, its gradient gives a force, and particles are updated under that force alone ΓÇö no hand-designed repulsion, attraction, or
phasor term. The network is trained by backpropagating a spectral-matching loss through the entire unrolled simulation.
This is simple as a concept, but not a foregone conclusion as a result. The central open question, flagged throughout this document, is whether a spectral-
matching loss alone is sufficient to produce a well-spaced point distribution, or whether it has a structural blind spot that requires an explicit spatial-
regularity loss to close. Section 6 works through why that blind spot exists and how to close it. The recommended path is to start with the minimum viable
loss (spectral + slope + one spacing term), validate the two non-negotiable gradient checks before training anything, and only add further metrics
(anisotropy, mean spacing, discrepancy) reactively, in response to a diagnosed failure ΓÇö not preemptively.
1. Research Objective
Develop a neural energy-based particle system that synthesizes 2-D point distributions with a user-specified frequency-domain spectrum, generalizing
continuously across the blue/pink/red/white/violet noise family rather than requiring a separately hand-tuned or separately optimized system per noise
color.
Primary hypothesis: A neural pairwise interaction energy, embedded inside a physically-motivated particle dynamical system (positions evolving under
the negative gradient of a learned energy), can learn to reproduce and interpolate across an entire family of target power spectra ΓÇö while remaining
interpretable, provably stable in the conservative case, and cheap to evaluate at inference time compared to per-target classical optimization.
This is a standalone system: it does not depend on, extend, or preserve any pre-existing hand-designed dynamical system. Any short-range regularization
needed to prevent particle collapse is either learned by the network itself or included as a minimal, clearly-labeled numerical stabilizer.
2. Positioning Relative to Prior Work

--- PAGE BREAK ---
Three adjacent bodies of work need to be addressed before implementation begins, since each offers a plausible alternative solution to the same problem.
2.1 Classical continuous spectral control
Spectral/Fourier-domain optimization of blue-noise and 1/f^╬▓ point sets, Lloyd-relaxation and capacity-constrained variants, and direct particle-position
optimization against a log-PSD loss can already hit arbitrary target slopes without any learned component. This is the most important comparison in the
whole proposal: if a classical per-target optimizer matches the neural modelΓÇÖs quality at comparable cost, ΓÇ£continuous controlΓÇ¥ alone is not a sufficient
contribution.
The differentiating claim must be one of: - Generalization across spectra without per-target re-optimization (train once, infer instantly for any new ╬│) -
A provable stability guarantee that ad hoc classical optimizers lack (Section 11) - A small, interpretable model versus an iterative per-target solve
Pick one as the headline claim early ΓÇö it shapes what gets emphasized in the writeup and which ablations matter most.
2.2 Learned point-process and blue-noise generation
GAN- and diffusion-based point/sample generators and learned optimal-transport samplers already produce blue-noise-like distributions. This proposalΓÇÖs
system should be distinguished on mechanism ΓÇö an interpretable energy embedded in a dynamical system, not a black-box generator ΓÇö and that
mechanism should be shown to buy something: controllability, the stability argument in Section 11, and a smaller model, rather than being justified on raw
sample quality alone.
2.3 Neural interaction-energy / neural force fields
Learned pairwise energies and neural force fields have precedent in particle simulation and molecular dynamics (permutation-invariant pairwise energies,
energy-conserving neural ODEs). This lineage should be cited to strengthen Stage 4ΓÇô5 below; the novelty is the application to spectral point synthesis and
conditioning on a target spectrum, not the energy-gradient force construction itself.
Action item before Stage 1: run a structured literature search (suggested terms: ΓÇ£continuous power spectrum point distribution optimization,ΓÇ¥ ΓÇ£neural
blue noise generation,ΓÇ¥ ΓÇ£learned point process sampling,ΓÇ¥ ΓÇ£differentiable particle simulation neural force field,ΓÇ¥ ΓÇ£energy-based point set optimization
graphicsΓÇ¥) and produce a short related-work table: method, mechanism, spectral controllability, reported cost. This determines the comparison baseline
target in Section 6.3.

5. Staged Implementation Plan
Stage 0 ΓÇö Reference Distributions and Baseline Metrics
Define reference/sanity-check distributions (Poisson-disk sampling, jittered grids, pure random) with known or well-characterized spectral properties.
These serve as ground truth for validating the spectral analyzer in Stage 3, and as the ΓÇ£known spectraΓÇ¥ row of the experimental matrix (Section 7). Record
baseline runtime and peak GPU memory at N=256 and N=512 ΓÇö this becomes the reference point for the compute budget in Section 6.
Stage 1 ΓÇö Define the Initial Learning Problem
2-D square domain, N=256 or N=512. First target family: power-law spectrum P*(f) = C┬╖f^╬│, ╬│ Γêê approximately [-2, +2], covering red, pink, white, blue,
and violet-like regimes. Do not begin with arbitrary PSDs, anisotropy, or spatially varying targets.
Stage 2 ΓÇö Differentiable Particle Representation
State X = {x_i}, each x_i a 2-D position. Implement the full simulation in PyTorch or JAX so gradients pass from the final spectral loss to the neural
parameters. Use a simple fixed-step Euler or semi-implicit update first.
Stage 3 ΓÇö Build and Validate the Spectral Analyzer
Rasterize particle positions into a density image D(x,y) using a soft Gaussian splat kernel (not hard/nearest-pixel splatting, which gives zero or
noisy position gradients).
Compute D╠é  = FFT(D), P(u,v) = |D╠é (u,v)|┬▓, radial frequency f = ΓêÜ(u┬▓+v┬▓), radially average to P(f), fit log-log slope to estimate ╬│╠é.
Validation requirement: verify the analyzer against Stage 0ΓÇÖs known reference distributions, and run a finite-difference gradient check on ΓêéD/
Γêéx_i before any training begins. This must pass before Stage 4.
Stage 4 ΓÇö Neural Pairwise Energy
For each particle pair: r_ij = ||x_i - x_j||, e_ij = NN_╬╕(r_ij, ╬│), E_╬╕(X,╬│) = ╬ú_{i<j} e_ij. The sum over unordered pairs makes the formulation permutation-
invariant. The network learns a distance-dependent interaction potential conditioned on the target spectrum. No hand-designed repulsion/attraction/phasor
term is assumed present.
Architecture decision ΓÇö free MLP vs. a built-in divergence prior. A fully unconstrained MLP(r) has no structural guarantee against particles
occupying the same point: unlike classical potentials (1/r, 1/r┬▓) that are built to diverge as rΓåÆ0, a plain MLP can output a small, finite, non-repulsive value
at rΓëê0 if that minimizes the loss. Two options:
Approach
Trade-off
Fully free MLP(r)

Stage 5 ΓÇö Convert Energy to Neural Force
Predict energy rather than directly predicting forces: F_i^NN = -ΓêéE_╬╕/Γêéx_i, via automatic differentiation. This keeps the learned force field consistent with
the learned energy and preserves the stability argument in Section 11.
Stage 6 ΓÇö Particle Dynamics
x_i(t+1) = x_i(t) + ╬öt┬╖F_i^NN. If early experiments show particle collapse or degenerate clustering under the spectral loss alone, add a minimal, fixed,
clearly-labeled stabilizing repulsion term (e.g., a short-range inverse-square push) ΓÇö but treat this as a fallback to test against, not a default. The first
experiments should attempt training with no hand-designed term at all, to test whether spacing regularity emerges purely from the spectral + spacing loss
(this is contribution C5 and a natural ablation).

CV_NND(X_T) ΓÇö coefficient of variation of nearest-neighbor distances. Chosen as the single primary spatial-quality term because it is cheap,
differentiable, and directly interpretable (lower CV = more regular spacing). The pair-correlation function g(r) is reserved as an evaluation-time metric
(Section 9), not a training loss, since it is harder to differentiate through reliably.
Full objective (minimum viable version): L = ╬╗_s┬╖L_spec + ╬╗_╬│┬╖L_╬│ + ╬╗_q┬╖L_spacing
Why L_spacing is structurally necessary, not a nice-to-have. The spectral loss is measured through a rasterized density image, produced by splatting
particles with a Gaussian kernel of width ╧â. That kernel acts as a low-pass filter: any structure finer than ╧â ΓÇö e.g. two particles collapsed almost on top of
each other ΓÇö is smeared into what looks like a single blob and is invisible to the FFT. This means the network can, in principle, satisfy L_spec by letting
particles collapse into tight sub-╧â clusters that are spaced apart at a coarser scale, producing a radial power spectrum that looks correct at the frequencies
the rasterizer can resolve while being spatially degenerate. L_spec cannot see this failure mode by construction ΓÇö it is a blind spot in the measurement,
not a training difficulty. L_spacing, computed directly on raw particle positions rather than through the rasterizer, is what closes this loophole, and should
be treated as required from the first experiment rather than added later if problems appear.
Diagnostic check (required at every milestone): after training, plot the pair-correlation function g(r) at small r. A spike near r=0 despite a low spectral
loss is the signature of sub-kernel collapse ΓÇö confirmed directly, rather than inferred. This check determines whether ╧â needs to shrink, whether ╬╗_q
needs to increase, or whether the divergence-prior fallback architecture (Stage 4) is needed.

6. Computational Budget & Training Strategy
Stage 11 identifies O(N┬▓) inference cost as a scalability concern, but the more immediate issue is training cost: Stage 8 backpropagates through T=50ΓÇô100
unrolled steps, each with an O(N┬▓) pairwise energy evaluation, for every training sample. Size this before Stage 4, not after hitting a wall.
6.1 Memory: truncated backpropagation through time
Full backprop through 50ΓÇô100 steps with an autograd graph over all pairwise interactions at N=256ΓÇô512 is likely memory-heavy on a single GPU. Plan
for either gradient checkpointing (recompute forward activations during the backward pass) or truncated BPTT (backpropagate through a sliding
window of the last k steps, run the rest under no_grad ). State the default choice for Stage 8, and record peak memory at N=256 and N=512 as part of
Stage 0ΓÇÖs baseline metrics.
6.2 Compute estimate
Before Stage 8, produce a rough GPU-hour estimate for milestone M1 (single ╬│, N=256) versus continuous-╬│ training (N=256ΓÇô512) versus the local-
energy milestone (N=4096). This drives whether Stage 11ΓÇÖs local-energy model should be pulled earlier if the full pairwise version is too slow to iterate
on.
6.3 Comparison Baseline (required)
Add a non-neural continuous-spectrum baseline to the experimental matrix: a classical per-target optimizer ΓÇö gradient-based or relaxation-based direct
particle-position optimization against the log-PSD loss, run separately for each test ╬│. Compare against the neural model on:
a. final spectral/spatial quality at matched ╬│
b. wall-clock cost per new target ╬│ ΓÇö the classical baseline pays this cost per target; the neural model pays it once at training time and should
be near-instant at inference
This is what turns ΓÇ£continuous spectral controlΓÇ¥ into a quantified claim rather than an assertion, and ΓÇö given there is no Paper 1 to fall back on for
credibility ΓÇö this comparison now carries more of the paperΓÇÖs weight than it would in an extension paper.

6.5 Domain Boundary Fix
Choose based on whether periodicity is essential to the desired spectral behavior:
Minimum-image convention (preferred if periodicity matters): keep wrapping, but compute pairwise distances using the wrapped/shortest-path
difference. Gradients pass through cleanly except at the measure-zero instant of an actual wrap event. Standard in periodic-boundary molecular
dynamics.

--- PAGE BREAK ---
Soft boundary force (simpler, if periodicity is not essential): replace hard teleportation with a smooth repulsive force near the edge, keeping
particles inside without wrapping. Removes the discontinuity entirely at the cost of true periodicity.
Straight-through estimator (fastest patch, use with caution): exact wrap in the forward pass, identity in the backward pass. Introduces small,
usually tolerable gradient noise; note explicitly as an approximation in ablations if used.
Required check: run the Stage 3 finite-difference gradient check specifically on a particle near the boundary, both before and after applying the fix. A
passing check away from the boundary but failing near it is the signature of this exact problem.

11. Theoretical Analysis
For the simplified conservative system dX/dt = ΓêÆΓêçE_╬╕(X), the energy satisfies dE/dt = ΓêÆΓÇûΓêçE_╬╕ΓÇû┬▓ Γëñ 0. This gives a stability interpretation for the learned
energy component, and is a central piece of the differentiation argument in Section 2.2 ΓÇö it is a guarantee classical per-target optimizers and black-box
generators typically do not offer. The complete system includes discrete integration and possibly a stabilizing term, so global convergence should not be
claimed without further proof; state this limitation explicitly.
